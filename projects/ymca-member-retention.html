The question

YMCA of the North wanted to know which early behaviors predict whether a new member is still there a year later, and which groups of members deserve attention first. Membership history from 2022 to 2025 lived in two systems, Personify and Salesforce, and the migration between them meant one person could appear under two IDs. Until those records were reconciled, retention could not be measured reliably, let alone predicted.

I worked on this as part of a five-person Carlson Analytics Lab team in Summer 2026. I was the Visuals / Deliverable Lead and owned the evaluation and QA of the churn models; I also worked on the data pipeline.

Data

33 raw extracts from the two systems: facility visits (about 20.8M rows) and program and membership enrollments (about 4.9M rows), 2022–2025, de-identified before analysis (birth dates reduced to year, ZIP codes to five digits). Quality issues included an ID crosswalk that at first matched only about half of members across systems, a 2025 migration that left branch IDs null and had to be backfilled from branch names, ethnicity unknown for roughly 50% of records, and open-ended enrollments where a missing end date meant “active”. No raw client data or personal data appears in this write-up.

Method

We consolidated the extracts into a five-phase DuckDB pipeline: ingest, crosswalk, visit aggregation, churn logic, and assembly. The crosswalk used a two-key matching strategy, which raised the cross-system ID match rate from 50% to 79%. The assembled layer covers 539K members and 9.4M monthly engagement records; the monthly table is queried in DuckDB only, because it is too large to load into pandas comfortably.

Churn was defined per membership instance with a 40-day grace period, and retention as one minus churn. For segmentation the team ran KMeans with k=4 on four lifetime features (visits per active month, unique branches visited, program categories, active months), with k chosen for interpretability; churn was used only afterwards to describe each segment.

I owned the evaluation and QA of the churn MVP. That meant time-based, member-grouped splits, a leakage audit that caught a member-level “returned after first visit” flag and replaced it with a clean 60-day proxy, and a rule that a validation cohort with zero churn positives gets no reported AUC. I also worked on the Power BI dashboard and built the client-facing deliverables.

Results

  • 539K members and 9.4M engagement records consolidated; the ID match rate improved from 50% to 79%.
  • Four behavior-based segments across 336K members; annual retention ranged from 60% in the minimal-engagement group to 89% among loyal regulars.
  • The minimal-engagement group was the largest segment, so it was flagged for early intervention.
  • The first-60-day membership churn model reached a validation ROC-AUC of about 0.64. Early visits were a real but weak signal once leakage was removed, and age at signup was the strongest clean predictor.

What it changed

At midterm the client told us they wanted operational decisions, not a higher AUC. The final deliverable followed one thread: risk signal, member segment, program pathway, outreach action. It included an outreach framework that maps risk tier and segment to a recommended contact action, a Power BI dashboard with an annual membership overview and program pathways, a final presentation, and a knowledge-transfer document for YMCA staff.

Limitations and next steps

Segmentation uses lifetime features, so it describes members rather than warning about them early. Cross-year comparisons into 2025 are distorted by the system migration, because returning members reappear under new IDs. Retention differences between segments are associations, not effects of any intervention. Next steps the team proposed: a next-60-day reactive model, one settled population definition for segmentation, and a program-category dataset for pathway analysis.
