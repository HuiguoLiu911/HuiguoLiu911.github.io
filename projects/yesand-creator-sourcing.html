The question

yes& is an AI camera and creative app that wanted to grow on TikTok through three kinds of creators: KOLs for paid reach, KOCs who already make AI content and would promote for credits, and small UGC creators who could post daily on dedicated accounts. The brief asked for a repeatable way to go from keywords to tiered creator tables with view metrics the team could act on, and to know which keywords were worth the money.

Data

Everything came from Apify TikTok actors: keyword searches returning videos with author bios, and profile pulls returning each creator’s recent 30 videos. Over three pipeline generations in August 2026 I accumulated a store of 2,791 creator profiles and 49,197 videos, plus five legacy long lists from earlier runs. The data is noisy in predictable ways: search hits carry only one or two videos per creator (biased toward viral posts), public emails appear in only a small share of bios, and the profile actor’s date parameters behave inversely to their names. Creator contact details are kept out of version control.

Method

The final pipeline (the third generation) is a four-step CLI plus a Streamlit browser:

  1. Search: keywords to a long list, with two hard conditions, at least 1,000 followers and a public email extracted from the bio by regex, including de-obfuscated “(at)/(dot)” forms. Everyone seen is kept in a second file with the reason they failed, so nothing is silently dropped.
  2. Tier by followers: KOL at 10,000 and above, KOC 3,000–10,000, UGC 1,000–3,000.
  3. Pull view counts, store first. Any step that costs money prints an estimate and stops unless explicitly confirmed; creators already in the store cost nothing.
  4. Tables per tier with two specified metrics, locked by tests: average views after removing videos above Q3 + 3×IQR, and the floor-5 average (mean of the five lowest). A creator with fewer than five videos pulled (when the account has more) is marked “insufficient data” rather than judged. KOC adds a views-to-followers ratio and an AI-content signal from bios and hashtags; UGC adds a viral flag.

The first generation had been a keyword-to-email list builder with a permanent master list so the same creator is never sold twice; the second measured creators on recent performance rather than follower count. I imported all of their data into the final store so no scrape had to be repeated.

Results

  • Long list of 8,562 creators meeting the hard conditions, from 16,181 seen; tiering of a 7,947-creator run gave KOL 4,347, KOC 1,653, UGC 1,947.
  • Passed candidates: 96 KOL, 25 KOC, 240 UGC, each with an all-candidates file explaining rejections.
  • Keyword yield: public-email rates by search keyword ranged from 1.4% to 19%, which answered the client’s key question about whether email could be a hard filter.
  • Pilot pull of 50 creators succeeded for 48 with 0 failures; the KOC tier passed none because the legacy lists lacked AI-content signals, which showed KOC candidates must come from AI-specific keywords.
  • Streamlit app lets a reviewer filter by tier, email, AI signal, follower band and views, see per-creator view distributions, and export the current selection.

What it changed

The tables and app became the team’s working list for outreach, and the keyword-yield file replaced guesswork about where to spend search budget. The pilot result redirected KOC sourcing toward AI and app keywords instead of the existing fashion and lifestyle lists.

Limitations and next steps

Average views are computed from the most recent 30 videos, not a creator’s full history, and search-only creators often lack enough videos to judge. Requiring a public email cuts most candidates, so the open business decision is whether to relax it in favor of direct messages. Next steps are a full KOC and UGC pull and testing a social-media finder to recover missing emails on a small sample first.
