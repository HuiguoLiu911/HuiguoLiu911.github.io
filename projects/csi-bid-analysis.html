Where did the bids miss the mark?

Central Specialties Inc · Construction-bidding strategy from two years of auction records

The question

Central Specialties Inc (CSI) is a Midwest road-construction contractor that bids on public highway lettings, mainly in Minnesota and North Dakota. It had two years of auction results and a direct question for our Carlson Analytics Lab team: where are our bids competitive, where are our estimates off, and how should that change what we bid on next?

I was one of five students on the engagement. I cleaned and classified the records, and built the Tableau dashboard the recommendations were presented from.

Data

1,661 road-construction auction records from August 2023 to September 2025 across six states: Iowa, Minnesota, Montana, North Dakota, Nebraska and South Dakota. Each record lists the project, the letting, the bidders and their bid amounts. CSI itself bid 608 times and won 114, an 18.75% win rate. The records are public letting results, but the client’s internal cost assumptions stayed with the client; nothing here reproduces them.

Method

The work was descriptive analytics and segmentation, done in Excel and Tableau. Cleaning and reshaping happened in Excel with Power Query and Pivot Tables: bidders were re-ranked 1 to 15 within each project, and counties were estimated from project coordinates. Because the raw data had no usable project-type field, I built a six-category classification from the material composition of each job (the share of asphalt mix tonnage and grading tonnage): shoulder and maintenance, grading and earthwork, maintenance and reconstruction, grading-heavy preparation, resurfacing and overlay, and heavy asphalt paving. The Tableau dashboard then compared bid accuracy (CSI’s bid against the winning bid), win rates by county and project type, and competitor patterns.

Results

  • Project type was the strongest indicator of over- or under-bidding. Grading and earthwork and shoulder and maintenance projects carried the largest gaps, about $1M average overbid, while resurfacing and overlay bids were the most accurate.
  • Minnesota bids were the most accurate; North Dakota and Iowa bids deviated more, so the Minnesota estimating approach became the benchmark for the other states.
  • County-level win rates varied widely, from about a third to 60% in the counties where CSI bid most often, which pointed to where its cost structure was genuinely competitive.
  • Some project types performed better in particular months, which suggested seasonal bidding windows.

What it changed

Three recommendations went to the client: align cost assumptions with project type instead of using one bidding formula, concentrate bids on the types and months that have historically won, and use the Minnesota framework to correct estimates in North Dakota and Iowa. Together they gave CSI a concrete starting point for lifting its 18.75% baseline win rate.

Limitations and next steps

The $1M figure is an observed average overbid, not a saving or a projected win-rate gain. The analysis is descriptive: it shows where bids missed, not why, because the records do not capture scope differences, subcontractor quotes or the client’s margin targets. A natural next step is a bid-calibration model that predicts the winning bid per project type and lets the estimator choose a margin against it.
