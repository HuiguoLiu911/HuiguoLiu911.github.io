Cutting photo calorie error from 27% to 19%

SnapCal · Weighed-meal eval, closed beta and unit economics for an AI calorie tracker

Real

In Oct 2026 I built SnapCal, a browser-only Cal AI–style tracker (live at huiguoliu911.github.io/snapcal/) where Claude vision returns schema-constrained per-item grams, calories, macros and confidence, edited grams rescale the numbers, goals use Mifflin–St Jeor, a 50-food list works offline, data stays in localStorage, and accuracy and usage were never measured.

Business context

Photo calorie trackers sell one promise: point the camera at the plate and skip the weighing. SnapCal already returns schema-constrained items with grams, calories, macros and a confidence level that the user can rescale.

The users are people trying to lose or hold weight who quit manual logging around week two. A subscription of about $5 a month only works if they trust the estimate enough to keep logging and their scans cost well under the net price.

The problem

Nobody, including me, knew how wrong SnapCal is. Systematic under-counting is worse than noise: a 500 kcal deficit on paper may be maintenance in reality, and the user blames themselves when the scale doesn’t move.

The north-star KPI is weekly logging users: the share of activated users who log at least one meal on 4 or more days in a calendar week. Guardrails, each with a threshold set before any testing:

  • Per-meal kcal MAPE on held-out weighed meals: mean |estimate − truth| / truth. Release bar 20% or lower.
  • Signed bias: mean (estimate − truth) / truth, within ±8%.
  • p95 scan latency, upload to items on screen: under 9 s.
  • Mean model cost per active user-month: under $1.50.

The prototype could answer none of these: no ground truth, no event logging, and an API key stored in each user’s browser.

What I built

A weighed-meal eval set. 120 phone-photographed meals, each shot plain and with a hand beside the plate: 84 home-cooked, 36 restaurant or takeout. For home meals I weighed every raw ingredient, oil included, on a 1 g scale, mapped it to USDA FoodData Central, then weighed the finished dish to get the plated share. Restaurant meals were weighed per component, so their truth is noisier (about ±10%) and reported separately. 40 meals formed a dev split; the other 80 stayed locked until the final comparison.

Prompt and capture variants. V0 is the shipped single prompt. V1 makes the model first list items, likely fats and sauces, and the visual cue behind each portion, then estimate, in one call with the same schema. V2 is V1 on the hand-in-frame photo; V3 is V2 at medium effort.

I rejected a fine-tuned food classifier plus nutrition lookup: it names foods well, but portion size is the bigger error source, and it needs thousands of labeled plates I don’t have. Multi-angle capture adds friction exactly where the product promises to remove it; a hand in frame is the cheaper scale reference.

A key proxy. A Cloudflare Worker now holds the API key. It accepts only JPEGs under 400 KB with a signed invite token, enforces per-user limits (3 scans a minute, 150 a month) and a global daily spend cap with a kill switch, and forwards a fixed server-side prompt. The schema allows only food items, so the endpoint is useless as a free vision API. Photos are never stored; the event log keeps tokens, latency, confidence and edits against an anonymous ID.

Results

V1 against V0 is a paired difference of −5.8 pp (CI −8.9 to −2.6), mostly from fats: oil, butter or sauce was in 52 of 80 meals, and V1 listed it in 37 against V0’s 21. The hand took off another 3.0 pp (CI −5.3 to −0.8). V2 clears the 20% bar on its point estimate, not its upper bound of 22.1%. Medium effort disappointed: 0.7 pp better than V2 (CI −2.6 to +1.3) at 1.8× the cost, with p95 over the latency guardrail.

By meal type, V2 scored 11.5% on home plated meals (n=34), 19.8% on restaurant (n=24), 26.9% on mixed dishes (n=14) and 30.2% on drinks (n=8). The confidence field tracked error: item-level MAPE was 41% on the 58 items flagged low, 14% on the 151 flagged high.

Closed beta, 4 weeks. In the simulated beta I invited 220 people from a waitlist of about 1,100 on one day; the 196 who set a goal are the analysis population. Activation (3 meals logged within 48 hours) was 63% (124/196), D7 retention (a meal on days 5–7) 46% (90/196) and D28 27% (53/196). Weekly logging users fell from 58% of activated users in week 1 (72/124) to 39% in week 4 (48/124). Everyone ran V1; the hand hint shipped in week 3 but appeared in only 34% of later photos, which puts compliance-weighted field MAPE near 20.6%, over the bar. The 18.6% is what the prompt can do, not what a typical user gets.

A/B test. Users were randomized 50/50 at signup. Arm A kept the shipped sheet: confidence as plain text, typed grams. Arm B showed colored confidence chips, low-confidence items first, and a portion slider. The primary metric was the share of AI-logged meals edited before saving, a process metric chosen because 196 users cannot power retention; a weighed audit would check whether editing improved accuracy. Guardrails: median time to save (limit +5 s) and net logged kcal versus the model’s estimate (limit ±3%). With about 24 meals per user and an assumed intra-user correlation of 0.15, the MDE was near 7 pp (α=0.05, power 0.8). Fixed horizon, one analysis at day 28.

B raised the edit rate from 17.6% (2,310 meals, 98 users) to 29.4% (2,452 meals, 98 users): +11.8 pp, cluster-bootstrap CI +6.0 to +17.4, wider than planned because the observed correlation was 0.21. Novelty explains part of it; the gap shrank from +15 pp in week 1 to +9 pp in weeks 3–4.

Both guardrails broke. Median time to save rose from 14 s to 20 s, and slider edits skewed downward, leaving B’s logged calories 4% below the model’s estimate and deepening the under-count. A 66-meal audit (22 volunteers, three weighed meals each) favored B (post-edit MAPE 20% → 16%, against 20% → 19% in A), but not significantly (about −3 pp, CI −7.1 to +1.4), and volunteers who weigh food are likely the careful editors. D28 was 28.6% (28/98) in B and 25.5% (25/98) in A, an interval of −9.3 to +15.5 pp that rules nothing in or out.

Business impact

Unit economics. The 53 users still logging at D28 averaged 38 scans that month (median 31, p90 92). At 1.7¢ a scan plus 4% retries and about 4¢ of proxy overhead, that is roughly $0.71 per active user-month, $1.67 at the p90. A $4.99 subscription nets about $4.24 after a 15% store fee, so model cost is about 17% of revenue and the 150-scan cap bounds the worst case near $2.70. Contribution is about $3.50 per paying user-month; if payers stayed four months, an untested assumption, acquisition must cost under about $14 per payer. Willingness to pay is the largest unknown.

Decisions. Ship V2 at low effort and move the hand hint from a tooltip into the camera overlay, since at 34% compliance most users still get V1 accuracy. Medium effort stays off. Keep the chips, which got people checking, but add a one-tap “add oil” chip so the easiest edit pushes against the under-count, and alert if net edits drift past −3%. A slower save is acceptable; edits that deepen the bias are not.

Weekly logging users is the number that has to hold, and nothing here shows the chips move it. Powering +5 pp on week-4 weekly logging (39% baseline) takes about 1,500 activated users per arm, roughly 4,800 signups, over four times the waitlist. So the next step is a fake-door price page for the ~880 uninvited waitlist users: enough intent funds paid acquisition for that test, too little keeps SnapCal a free tool.

Technical deep dive

Data and pipeline. Eval rows hold the photo pair, items with grams and FDC IDs, and computed truth. A Python harness calls the app’s own proxy endpoint, so the tested prompt is the shipping one, and stores raw JSON for re-scoring.

Model and rules design. One schema-constrained call, so there is no prose to parse. Drinks trigger a size and milk question, because the photo cannot answer either. A malformed or timed-out response is retried once; a second failure drops the user into text entry against the 50-food offline list.

Metric definitions. MAPE is per meal, because users act on the meal total, and bias sits beside it because MAPE hides direction. Item recall counts ground-truth items worth at least 5% of meal kcal that the model named, matched by hand.

Evaluation. Splits are by meal, both photos of a meal stay together, and the 80 test meals were not opened during iteration. Relabeling 20 meals two weeks later moved truth kcal by a median 4%, a floor on what counts as a real difference. The baseline is the shipped prompt. Meal types are imbalanced, so I report by type, not just pooled.

Failure cases.

  • Mixed dishes (curries, chili, casseroles): 26.9% MAPE. Dissolved fat is invisible, so the model assumes a typical recipe.
  • Drinks: 30.2%. A whole-milk latte with syrup looks identical to a skim one without.
  • Oversized restaurant plates: estimates regress toward a typical portion, leaving −11% bias on restaurant meals even with a hand in frame.

What I’d optimize next. Barcode lookup for packaged food, a “how was this cooked?” chip for mixed dishes, and per-user calibration once someone has weighed a few meals.

If I made this real

  • Weigh and photograph the 120 meals for real, with a second labeler on a subset.
  • Run V0–V3 on the held-out set and publish the real MAPE, bias and cost.
  • Deploy the proxy with rate limits, a spend cap and anonymous event logging.
  • Get 30 outside testers for two weeks to measure activation, edit rate and hand-hint compliance.
  • Show the waitlist a price page for a first willingness-to-pay signal.
