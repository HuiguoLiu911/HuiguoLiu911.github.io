Business context

In this scenario an analytics consulting lab starts every engagement with an intake packet: a kickoff transcript, the sponsor’s brief, and sometimes a data dictionary. Within a week a consultant turns it into a charter with six fields: objectives, KPI definitions, data sources, data-access constraints, risks and open questions. A manager reviews it, then the sponsor confirms or corrects it in a meeting.

About 30 engagements a semester, many re-scoped once or twice, add up to roughly 75 charter drafts a term and over 200 consultant-hours. The bigger cost is downstream: a charter that misstates a KPI or assumes data the client can’t share gets corrected at the confirmation meeting, and the data request slips a week.

The problem

I had built two pieces of this as course prototypes: one tied résumé–job fit claims to retrieved résumé chunks, the other turned sponsor notes into a kickoff package with guardrails and a repair pass. Neither was ever evaluated. The question was whether one grounded extraction workflow could produce a charter a consultant finishes faster, without content the client never said.

  • North star: active drafting time per charter, in consultant hours from opening the packet to submitting for manager review. Both pilot arms used the same app (manual arm with extraction off), so one timer measured both.
  • Guardrail: manager rework rate. Share of drafts returned for scope, KPI or data changes; copyedits don’t count.
  • Guardrail: hallucinated items. Generated items with no supporting span anywhere in the packet, over all generated items.
  • Guardrail: citation groundedness. Share of claims whose cited span supports them, on a three-level rubric where “partial” counts as unsupported.

In the manual process, a manager reading “reduce churn by 5%” could not tell whether the sponsor said 5% or the consultant assumed it.

What I built

Every item keeps its span IDs through four stages.

Ingest. Files are parsed and redacted (regex for emails and phones, spaCy NER for names, replaced with role tokens like [SPONSOR_1]), split into ~150-token spans on speaker turns, and embedded into a per-engagement FAISS index.

Extract. One structured-JSON call per field, each with its own retrieval query; every item must cite one to three span IDs. Source weighting carries over from the intake prototype: transcript over brief over earlier notes over data dictionary.

Verify. A second gpt-4.1-mini pass reads each claim beside its cited span and labels it supported, partial or unsupported. Unsupported items move to an “Unverified” tray instead of being deleted; partial ones stay with an amber flag.

Review. A Streamlit screen lists each field’s items; clicking one highlights its span in the source. The consultant accepts, edits or rejects each item, SQLite logs every action with before and after text, and export stays locked until every field is reviewed.

I rejected fine-tuning because 60 gold charters can evaluate a model but not train one, lab agreements forbid training on client material, and the failure was grounding rather than tone. I rejected a general chat assistant, which consultants already pasted notes into, because it gave no span links, no fixed schema and nothing a manager could audit, and it sent client text outside the lab’s tenant.

Calls go to an Azure OpenAI deployment inside that tenant, redaction runs before embedding, and packets are deleted 90 days after an engagement closes. A packet uses about 60k input and 8k output tokens (six extraction calls, ~15 verify calls): roughly $0.04 at gpt-4.1-mini list prices, p50 latency 47 seconds, run on upload so nobody waits.

The schema is a config file, so a résumé–JD fit template runs on the same engine.

Results

Offline comparison. The eval set was 60 historical packets with consultant-written gold charters, split by date: the 20 oldest for prompt iteration and few-shot examples, the 40 newest held out and scored once per variant after prompts were frozen. Intervals are 95% packet-level bootstrap CIs.

The verify pass did not improve F1. It moved 37 of B’s items to the tray: 15 hallucinations, 16 correct items citing the wrong span, and 6 supported items absent from gold. Pooled precision rose from 0.65 to 0.67; recall fell from 0.76 to 0.72. I took that trade because a missing item takes a minute to restore from the tray, while an unsupported one is expensive once a sponsor reads it. The groundedness gain (paired bootstrap +0.07, CI +0.04 to +0.10) is partly flattered because verifier and judge do similar work; on the human-labeled subsample the B-to-C gap was 0.05.

Simulated consultant pilot. Over 10 weeks, 12 consultants handled 54 new packets. The first week’s 6 were warm-up and excluded; the other 48 were randomized within consultant, two per arm each, 24 per arm. With a timesheet SD of about 1.2 hours, detecting a 1-hour difference at α = 0.05 and power 0.8 needs 23 per arm. The analysis ran once, at the end.

  • Drafting time: 3.2 h manual vs 1.3 h copilot, −1.9 h (95% CI −2.5 to −1.3) with consultant fixed effects. In-tool review took a median 34 minutes, about 2.3 minutes per item; the rest was reading the packet and finishing prose. Consultants worked both arms, so copilot habits could leak into manual drafts and shrink the measured gap.
  • Rework moved the wrong way: 7/24 (29%) copilot vs 5/24 (21%) manual, +8 pp (95% CI −16 to +33). Not significant, but the sample cannot rule out real harm. Five of the seven returns were flagged for KPI definitions.
  • Field acceptance (unchanged, or under 20% of characters edited; 352 items): objectives 36/43 (84%), open questions 57/72 (79%), data sources 38/58 (66%), risks 60/98 (61%), data-access constraints 14/34 (41%), KPI definitions 18/47 (38%).
  • Hallucinations reaching manager review: 1 of 352 (an assumed weekly data refresh), against about 7 expected at the offline 2.1% rate.

Both numbers sit far above my intake prototype memo’s assumptions (45–60 minutes manual, under 10 assisted), which were for tidy notes. Reading and review, not generation, set the floor.

Business impact

The decision would be to make variant C the default, with one untested change: KPI definitions and data-access constraints switch to “ask” mode, where the copilot drafts candidate definitions as questions for the sponsor. Those two fields had the lowest acceptance and caused five of seven returns.

My pre-set launch bar was at least 1 hour saved with rework no more than 10 pp worse on the point estimate. The pilot cleared time easily and rework only just, with an interval that can’t exclude harm, so launch would be conditional.

At ~75 drafts a term, 1.9 hours each is about 140 consultant-hours (100–190 across the CI). Netting out extra rework (8 pp of 75 is ~6 more returns at ~1.5 hours) leaves about 130. Model spend is about $3 a term, so the economics rest on consultant time and on rework falling. I did not measure calendar days to sponsor sign-off, where the downstream delay sits; that is the next metric to track.

Rollout: one term, all teams, rework tracked weekly. If 8 of any 20 consecutive drafts are returned (40%, against 21% manual), pause and audit them. That is a tripwire, not a significance test.

Technical deep dive

Data & pipeline

Packets averaged 9,800 tokens; gold charters averaged 14.5 items. Gold was written after later meetings, so 64 of 580 test gold items (11%) had no support in the packet. I excluded those from the recall denominator, leaving 516.

Prompt design

A KPI must state numerator, denominator and window, or return "definition": null plus an open question. Output is schema-validated JSON with one retry on parse failure (2.3% of calls).

Metric definitions

An item matches gold when embedding cosine is at least 0.80 and an LLM adjudicator agrees, with one-to-one Hungarian assignment; my hand audit of 150 matches agreed 91%. Macro-F1, because risks (4.1 items per packet) would swamp objectives (1.8) in a micro average. Groundedness is judged per claim by gpt-4.1, not the generator, to limit self-preference. Two consultants labeled 200 claims stratified by variant and field: human–human κ = 0.78, judge vs adjudicated label 89% agreement (κ = 0.71). The judge was lenient on paraphrases that harden commitments (“would like” became “will”), so true groundedness is probably about 2 pp lower.

Evaluation

Few-shot examples come only from dev packets, and CIs resample whole packets because claims within one are correlated. The single-prompt baseline mirrors what consultants already did in a chat tool.

Failure cases

  1. KPI definitions. Transcripts name metrics (“retention”) without a formula. The model stayed vague or invented a definition and cited the span that merely named the metric.
  2. Data-access constraints. Mentioned in passing (“we’d need IT sign-off for the POS data”), ranked low by retrieval, and any dataset mentioned was treated as available.
  3. Speaker attribution. Without speaker labels, the consultant’s own suggestions became sponsor objectives.

What I’d optimize next

Diarized speaker labels, a dedicated retrieval query for constraints, per-field confidence to sort the review queue, and a test set grown from logged edits.

If I made this real

  • Build a consented, redacted gold set of at least 40 packets with two annotators per charter; meanwhile, synthesize packets from the intake prototype’s samples.
  • Measure judge–human agreement on a fresh 200 claims before trusting any groundedness number.
  • Run the timing pilot with 24+ packets per arm, ask mode on, and the rework tripwire live.
  • Measure redaction recall for names and emails on 50 hand-annotated transcripts.
  • Replace token-based cost estimates with logged Azure usage.
