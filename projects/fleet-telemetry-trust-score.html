Business context

A school transportation operator runs bus, van and cab vendors on one routing platform. The GPS trail is the only objective record that a route ran. Parents watch buses on it, and vendors bill per trip, so a trip without a usable trail is paid on the vendor’s word.

The dataset covers two schools, 16 vendors, 433 vehicles and 14,859 trips over about 120 school days, with 2.35M GPS points. A one-time audit found that more than a quarter of trips had no GPS and about 35% could not be verified either way. This concept runs that logic daily for the coordinator who handles vendor issues.

The problem

Late buses get reported. Nobody, though, could say on the day which trips the data could vouch for, and a dead tracker surfaced days later through a parent complaint.

North-star KPI: share of scheduled trips that are unverifiable: zero pings, observed pings below 40% of the count the provider’s polling interval implies, or fewer than half of planned stops decidable. A stop is decidable when a ping lands inside its 80 m geofence or the trace never comes within 500 m of it.

Guardrails: - Alert precision: share of alerts the coordinator marks actionable in the resolution log. Floor 0.70. - Alert load: alerts per coordinator per school day. Cap 12; beyond that, review turns into skimming. - Mis-attributed penalties: share of scorecard REDs later traced to platform configuration. Target under 10%.

The audit graded each trip once. Nobody owned the follow-up, and it could not tell a broken device from a misconfigured route.

What I built

I designed a batch that runs at 09:30 and 17:30. It pulls the platform’s Postgres export into DuckDB, runs six checks per trip and scores two axes: data trust H (can we tell?) and execution E (did it run as planned?). Scores feed a coordinator alert queue and a weekly vendor scorecard. A typical alert reads Veh 214 · NO_LINK · AM · 0 pings, device online · owner: vendor B. The coordinator checks the map replay, calls the vendor or files a platform ticket, and logs cause, fix and time to close; that log recalibrates the weights.

The score keeps the audit’s formula: trust = (0.4 + 0.6·H) × E, with E a weighted mean of stop coverage, order, timing and path proximity. Trips with H below 0.4 get no execution grade and become data alerts for the vendor’s device owner, so a dead tracker never reads as bad driving.

I rejected two alternatives. An isolation forest on per-trip features would mostly learn that 60-second providers differ from 20-second ones, and when a third of trips are broken, missing GPS is not an anomaly. A contract scorecard also has to survive a vendor meeting: “four pings in 38 minutes against an expected 114” can be checked, an anomaly score of 0.83 cannot. Per-ping streaming would add infrastructure to gain a few hours; the batch already detects failures the same day and runs in under a minute on a small VM.

No rider data is read, stop coordinates stay in the database, and the coordinator reviews every alert before a vendor sees it.

Results

All numbers in this section come from a simulated pilot and are illustrative.

Setup. Six school weeks, four volunteer vendors (A–D), one coordinator, about 290 trips a week on 70 vehicles, many of them part-time vans and cabs. The other twelve vendors were scored silently as a concurrent comparison group. Vendors could not be randomized and volunteers are likely the keener ones, so this is pre/post with a shadow comparison; both groups were flat (±2 pp) over the four baseline weeks. One dead tracker spoils every trip on its vehicle, so CIs come from a cluster bootstrap over vehicles. They describe these four vendors only. Sizing: 1,160 baseline vs 580 pilot trips give a naive MDE of about 7 pp (α = 0.05, power 0.8), nearer 10 pp after clustering.

North star. Unverifiable share at pilot vendors fell from 36.2% (420/1,160, four baseline weeks) to 22.1% (128/580, weeks 5–6): −14.1 pp, 95% CI [−20.6, −7.4]. The shadow group went from 34.6% to 33.1% (n = 1,420 / 710). Difference-in-differences: −12.6 pp [−19.8, −5.1].

About 9 of the 14 points came from seven vehicles at vendors A and C, fixed in week 2: four trackers installed but never linked to the vehicle record, two stuck in a power-save mode pinging every five minutes, one mapped to the wrong bus after a swap.

Detection. In the eight pre-pilot weeks, 19 dead-tracker episodes at pilot vendors took a median of 4 school days to reach anyone (IQR 2–8). In the pilot, 17 of 23 were flagged the same day (74%, Wilson 95% CI 54–87%); the rest died after the 17:30 run or hit substitute vehicles. Median time to repair barely moved (2.5 → 2 days); repairs still wait for a vendor technician.

Where it didn’t work. Vendor D stayed near 30%. Its provider polls every 60 seconds, so a bus at 30 km/h moves about 500 m between pings and often leaves none inside an 80 m geofence. Those stops are undecidable; only faster polling helps. Mis-attributed penalties also missed target: 7 of 58 scorecard REDs (12%) traced to stop sequences misconfigured in the platform.

Alert guardrails. Weeks 1–2 broke the cap at 19 alerts a day with 64% precision (122/190), mostly repeats on issues already open. After deduplicating to one alert per vehicle-day and muting open issues, weeks 3–6 averaged 9 a day at 81% precision (146/180); pooled, 72% (268/370). At about four minutes per alert, that is 35 coordinator-minutes a day instead of about 75.

Business impact

The results support one contract decision and defer another.

Verifiability goes into vendor contracts: vendors control it, and pilot vendors fixed device faults within days of being told. None reached 85% verifiable by week 6 (A came closest at 82%), so the SLA would phase in at 80% for the first term and 85% after, with dead trackers re-paired within one school day and polling of 30 seconds or faster in renewals. That clause is the only lever for vendor D.

The execution score stays advisory: at 12% mis-attributed REDs, vendors would be penalized for the platform’s own sequencing errors.

Verifiability is the north star because execution grades mean nothing on trips the data cannot vouch for; each point removed is about three more invoiced trips a week, per 290, that can be checked against a trace. The other twelve vendors would join in two waves of six, each after two weeks of silent scoring to size alert volume; a second coordinator joins only if deduplicated load stays above 12 a day. Cadence: queue after each batch, scorecards on Mondays, monthly re-fit from the resolution log.

Technical deep dive

Data & pipeline. Trips, pings, stops, bell times and the vehicle-device mapping come from the Postgres export, about 20K pings a day. Checks are DuckDB SQL with Haversine distances to geofences (80 m; schools 300 m). Late pings are re-scored next batch; a failed batch retries twice, then shows a “scores stale” banner.

Rules design. H = observed/expected pings × share of trip time outside gaps, capped at 0.3 when frozen or jump flags fire. - Gap: interval over 3× polling and over 5 minutes. - Frozen: five or more identical fixes outside a stop dwell. - Impossible jump: implied speed over 130 km/h. - Geofence: a ping inside the radius during the stop window; no ping inside but a trace within 500 m is undecidable, not missed. - Order: Spearman correlation of planned vs observed rank, direction-aware so the school is last on morning trips (the audit once had this reversed). - Arrival: minutes before the bell.

Calibration. Two raters labeled 312 trips from map replays, blind to the score (Cohen’s κ = 0.71; 49 disagreements adjudicated, mostly “deviation vs unverifiable” on 60-second trips). A 0.05-step grid search on the 200 baseline-week trips, maximizing RED precision at recall ≥ 0.65, set coverage 0.35, timing 0.25, order 0.20, proximity 0.20 and RED below 0.42.

Evaluation. The 112 test trips come from pilot weeks 1–2, labeled after the weights were frozen. RED precision was 0.78 (32/41, 95% CI 0.63–0.88) and recall 0.70 (32/46), against 0.58 (34/59) and 0.74 (34/46) for the audit’s grade-D rule: two more misses, but false alarms fall from 25 to 9. The sample over-represents RED candidates; reweighting by inverse sampling probability puts production precision near 0.71, in line with the 72% alert log. The score is a ranking, not a probability, so I report band rates: GREEN 2/32, YELLOW 12/39, RED 32/41.

Failure cases. 1. Substitute vehicles: the spare bus has no tracker, so the alert blamed a device instead of the assignment. It now has its own reason code. 2. Wrong device mapping: pings from another route produce a confident RED. Impossible jumps at trip start now force a data alert. 3. Misconfigured stop sequences: a route failing the order check identically every day would spam REDs. When 80% of a route’s trips fail alike, one route-audit ticket replaces the vendor alerts.

Next. Polling-aware geofence radii, then a gradient-boosted model tested against the rules on the same time split, once full rollout yields about 1,500 labeled outcomes a semester.

If I made this real

  • Rebuild the checks on the real export and backtest a semester of weekly scorecards.
  • Label 300 real trips from map replays with two reviewers; report κ and RED precision/recall against the audit’s score.
  • Run two weeks of silent scoring with real coordinators to measure alert load and precision.
  • Confirm root causes against vendor maintenance records instead of inferring them from ping patterns.
