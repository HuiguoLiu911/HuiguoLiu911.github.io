The question

Influencer marketing teams source creators by picking keywords, running a scraper, filtering the long list by follower count and average views, and reviewing the rest by hand. It looks like a search problem. For my LLM Business Application course project (Option 2: a business plan plus a working prototype) I argued it is really two problems, recall and precision, and that conflating them is why the long lists are bad. The use case came from my work with yes&, an AI camera app sourcing digital-collage, scrapbook and photo-dump creators on TikTok.

Data

The inputs were a 4,248-creator long list from earlier keyword scraping, a hand-curated gold set of 259 creators an expert had approved, and Apify-fetched profiles and recent posts for the candidates I chose to enrich. All creator identifiers, emails and raw payloads stay out of the repository; the pipeline also ships a synthetic fixture campaign whose handles are prefixed so demo data can never leak into a real run.

Method

I built a ten-stage Python pipeline where every threshold lives in a campaign YAML rather than in code: candidate generation (deliberately noisy), deduplication with provenance, gold-set coverage analysis, enrichment, feature engineering over a 90-day window with outlier-resistant statistics (bottom-5 average views, trimmed mean, view CV, median engagement), hard filters, embedding similarity against the gold set, an optional Claude semantic classifier on the top candidates only, final ranking, and evaluation. Scores are normalised by winsorised percentile rank so one viral creator cannot compress everyone else. Claude never does numeric filtering and never makes the accept/reject call; it converts content characteristics into structured scores with risk flags. A Streamlit review UI logs human decisions, which feed back into the gold set.

I ran six numbered experiments, each regenerable from cached intermediates.

Results

  • Recall: only 28 of 259 expert-approved creators (10.8%) appeared anywhere in the 4,248-creator scrape. 34 of 50 keywords produced zero approved creators.
  • Threshold validation: the five originally specified filters kept 6 of 241 enrichable expert picks (2.5%). The expert was selecting on engagement (median 8.93% vs 6.57%), not view volume.
  • Gold-set ablation: removing the examples changed 7 of the top 10 results (Spearman ρ = 0.435).
  • Learning curve: ranking stability flattened at about 100 examples; 25 examples buys roughly half of it.
  • Keyword re-targeting recovered 9 of 231 missing creators, lifting recall from 10.8% to 14.3%.
  • LLM ablation: Claude fit scores were negatively correlated with embedding similarity (r = −0.234, p = 0.019) because about 35% of the reference examples were off-target. 100 creators classified, 0 failures.
  • Total cloud spend for every result: $5.16.

What it changed

The report reframed the client’s sourcing problem from “scrape more” to “measure recall, derive thresholds from your own approved set, and treat review decisions as training data”. The measured thresholds replaced the intuition-based ones, and the contaminated gold set became a cleanup task with a “most suspicious first” sort in the review UI. The prototype runs end to end offline on fixtures with 167 passing tests.

Limitations and next steps

Precision@K and lift are not yet measurable because the only labelled creators are the all-positive gold set, so the reported 100% precision is circular. The gold set itself is about 35% contaminated, so every similarity number is measured against a partly wrong target. The system is metadata-first with no visual understanding. The experiments cannot be reproduced by a third party because the input data is proprietary. The immediate next step is cleaning the gold set and reviewing candidates so lift can be measured honestly.
