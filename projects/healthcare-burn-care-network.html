Where are the burn deserts?

HeatMap Hackathon 2026 · Mapping U.S. burn-care capacity and a 3-tier telemedicine referral network

The question

Severe burns are rare but time-critical, and burn-capable hospitals are concentrated in a few metropolitan areas. The hackathon brief asked a practical question: given the hospitals that exist today, where does burn care break down geographically, and what could close the gap without building new facilities?

Our five-person team entered the HeatMap Hackathon 2026 (presented by BData) with the theme “Bridging the Gap: Building a 3-Tier Burn Intervention & Telemedicine Network”. I worked on the data analysis and the visualizations.

Data

The National Injury Resource Database (NIRD): 635 trauma-capable hospitals described by 23 fields, including trauma level, bed counts, adult and pediatric burn capability, American Burn Association (ABA) verification, and location. The dataset is public and contains no patient records; all analysis was at the hospital level.

Method

In Python (pandas for the profiling, Plotly, Matplotlib and Seaborn for the maps and charts) I classified every hospital by its burn capability and rolled the counts up to the state level to find states with no dedicated burn beds. I then compared adult and pediatric capability side by side, and sized the hospitals that had trauma capacity but no burn program, because those were the candidates for a middle tier that could handle moderate burns with telemedicine support from a verified center.

Results

  • Of 635 trauma-capable hospitals, 120 have adult burn capability, 74 are ABA-verified burn centers, and 89 can treat pediatric burns, which is 25.8% fewer than the adult count.
  • The country has about 2,080 dedicated burn beds, 0.72% of total beds, and eight states have none at all: the “burn deserts”.
  • The proposed network has three tiers: 74 ABA-verified centers as hubs for severe and complex cases; 413 “sweet spot” trauma centers across 47 states (about 470 beds on average) as spokes that manage moderate burns with telemedicine guidance; and 148 community hospitals as access points for initial stabilization and transfer.

What it changed

The recommendation was deliberately infrastructure-light. Rather than proposing new burn units, it routes patients through hospitals that already exist and connects them to specialists through a telemedicine layer that scales at low marginal cost. For a moderate burn, keeping the patient at a spoke hospital instead of flying them to a hub avoids a projected $12K–$25K per helicopter transfer, and it shortens the time to first specialist contact in the states with no burn beds of their own.

Limitations and next steps

This was a hackathon analysis on hospital-level capacity data, not outcomes data, so it says where capacity is missing rather than how patients fare. Travel times were approximated by state rather than road distance, and the sweet-spot tier was defined by bed count and trauma level, not by staffing or burn-specific training. A next step would be to overlay drive-time isochrones and burn-incidence rates to prioritize which spokes to equip first.
