Tell drivers where demand is, and why

The question

Ride-hailing platforms constantly mismatch where drivers are and where riders want to be picked up. Large platforms solve this with opaque dispatch systems that move drivers around. Our Big Data Analytics team (Team 7, Spring 2026) asked a different question: could historical trip data produce simple, explainable nudges that tell a driver where demand has been strong, and why, while leaving the decision with the driver? Our framing was “ride-hail apps tell drivers where to go; we tell them why, and let them decide.” I owned Phase 5, the LLM nudge-generation layer, and co-owned the evaluation.

Data

NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission yellow-taxi trip records for January to March 2023: 9.4M trips in three monthly parquet files (about 145 MB compressed) plus the 265-zone taxi-zone lookup table. Cleaning kept trips inside the date range, dropped invalid zone IDs, fares below the $2.50 minimum and implausible distances, removed duplicates and normalized timestamps. We developed the pipeline on a 3,024-row sample notebook first, then ran it on the full three months.

Method

The pipeline has 11 stages and runs in Databricks with PySpark and Delta Lake: ingest the parquet files, clean, join to zone names, write cleaned trips to Delta, aggregate demand by zone, time bucket and day type, split January–February for ranking from March for validation, rank the top 5 high-confidence zones per window with PySpark window functions, write the ranked zones, generate one nudge per window, evaluate on the holdout, and plot five figures.

The ranking is heuristic, not a forecasting model: a demand score is each zone-window cell’s trip count divided by the average across zones in the same window, so 1.5 means 50% above average for that hour and day type. Cells with too few trips, too few observed days or a coefficient of variation above 2 are flagged low-confidence and never reach the LLM.

My Phase 5 work started with two hand-written “gold” nudges to fix tone, length and grounding before any API call. The prompt feeds gpt-4o-mini (temperature 0.3) a structured summary of the five ranked zones (zone, score, trip count, days observed) and a tight set of rules: one phrasing style (“about 4.6x busier than average”), at most two zones, cautious wording when the data is thin, no real-time framing, and no reasoning beyond the provided numbers. Each nudge is written to a Delta table and passes four automated gates: the top zone must be cited, no forbidden or fabricated words, under 60 words, and no raw decimal score copied into the text.

Results

  • On the full January–February data, the recommended zones for all 8 time windows (four time buckets by weekday or weekend) were top-demand zones in the March holdout: a 100% hit rate, five times the 20% random baseline.
  • 8 of 8 nudges passed every grounding check.
  • Validation was temporal: rank on January–February, test on March, so the result measures whether historical demand patterns held into a month the pipeline had not seen.

What it changed

Delivered a working end-to-end pipeline from raw parquet to driver message, a two-page handout, a flyer and poster, and an under-five-minute presentation. The design point we argued for is human-in-the-loop: the model explains where demand is historically strong instead of issuing directives, and every claim in a message traces back to a number in the ranked table.

Limitations and next steps

Heuristic ranking on three months of data captures stable patterns such as rush-hour airports and nightlife zones; it does not forecast events, weather or new demand. The evaluation checks whether recommended zones were busy, not whether a driver who followed a nudge earned more. Next steps: run on full-year data, add the Chicago taxi dataset as an external check, and test the nudges with drivers.
