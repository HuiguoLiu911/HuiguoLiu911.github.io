Predictive Analytics project, extended · risk score to retention-offer policy

Business context

The course project ended with a leaderboard number: about 0.84 out-of-fold AUC for ranking dissatisfied bank customers. A retention team cannot act on a ranking alone. Every month it decides whom an agent should call with a save offer, knowing that each call and fee waiver costs money and most people on any list were never going to leave.

Here the Kaggle data stands in for a simulated retail bank’s customer snapshot. The retention team owns the offer budget and agent hours; finance asks whether the offers paid for themselves. An offer to a content customer is pure cost, and a missed high-value customer takes years of margin with them.

The problem

AUC says the score orders customers well, not where to cut the list, and it treats rank 200 the same whether that customer holds $150 or $3,000 of future margin. The usual default, “call the top decile,” is a cutoff picked for convenience.

North-star metric: net retained value per scored customer = (incremental saves × 3-year CLV − offer cost) / customers scored, with “incremental” measured against a randomized holdout.

Guardrails: - Cost per incremental save: offer spend / ((control attrition − treated attrition) × treated n). - Complaint rate: inbound complaints within 56 days per offered customer, a cost of pressure the CLV model does not see. - Calibration error: expected calibration error (ECE, 15 equal-mass bins) of the probability that drives the rule, checked monthly, alert at 0.01.

The course pipeline output a rank blend, not a probability, and knew nothing about cost, value, or how many customers an offer keeps.

What I built

I kept the course model as the risk score and added calibration, an expected-value rule and a pilot design. Every number below comes from a policy simulator I wrote: given p, CLV, save rate s and offer cost c, it returns the offer list, net value and a bootstrap interval.

Calibration. The only probability-like output in the team pipeline was the downsampled XGBoost branch, which averaged 0.148 on the holdout against a 3.96% base rate. I fit an isotonic calibrator on the out-of-fold blend score, so it never sees rows the model trained on. Platt scaling reached ECE 0.008 against 0.005; I kept isotonic because the sigmoid drifted from observed rates in the top tail, where the offers go.

Decision rule. Offer if p × s × CLV − c > 0, where p stands in for the chance the customer leaves within 56 days without an offer. Simulated inputs: c = $25 ($10 of agent time plus a $40 fee waiver at an assumed 37.5% acceptance); CLV is 3-year margin, lognormal with median $310 and mean $510, capped at $5,000; s = 0.25. I drew CLV independent of the label. Tying it to var38, which looks like a balance field, would have built a guess about anonymized data into the headline.

Two alternatives lost. A cost-sensitive classifier on dollar-weighted labels folds the value assumptions into the model: every cost change means a retrain, and finance cannot audit the cutoff. Uplift modeling needs randomized treatment data, which Kaggle lacks. So s has to be measured, and the pilot randomizes 10% of EV-targeted customers into a no-offer holdout.

Operations. Scoring runs monthly. The under-23 eligibility rule applies first; the retention lead signs off on list size before the list reaches the agents’ CRM queue with two reason codes per customer. If agent hours run out before the EV bar does, the list becomes the top k by expected value.

Results

All results are illustrative. I split the 76,020 labeled rows 80/20, stratified: 60,816 for model development (the original 5-fold out-of-fold pipeline) and a locked holdout of 15,204 customers (604 positives, 3.97%), used once.

Calibration. Holdout AUC was 0.836; isotonic is monotone, so the ranking is untouched. Brier score went from 0.057 for the raw downsampled-branch probabilities to 0.034 (a constant 3.97% scores 0.038), and ECE from 0.104 to 0.005.

Policy comparison at the assumed s = 0.25 and c = $25, net value realized on the holdout: - No offers: $0. - Random 10%: −$30.3K. - Top decile by score: 1,520 offers, 298 dissatisfied reached (19.6% precision, 49% of positives), +$1.9K (bootstrap 95% CI −$5.5K to +$10.3K). The best score-only cutoff, around 5%, reached +$9.7K. - EV rule: 597 offers (3.9%), 129 dissatisfied reached (21.6% precision), +$23.2K (CI $15.1K to $32.1K).

Precision is almost the same; the EV list’s mean CLV was $1,540 against $533 for the top decile. The $23.2K also ran lucky: summing p × s × CLV − c over the same 597 customers gives an expected $18.6K, the figure I would show finance.

Sensitivity. Holding the s = 0.25 list fixed, the EV policy breaks even at a true s of 0.098; the top decile needs 0.238.

Simulated pilot. One scoring run on a simulated book flagged 7,000 customers: 6,300 offered, 700 held out, outcomes read once at day 56 by assignment (unreached customers stay treated). For power I assumed 19% control attrition (the EV list’s 21.6% dissatisfied rate, discounted for dissatisfied customers who stay) and a 25% relative reduction as MDE; at α = 0.05, power 0.8 and 9:1 allocation that needs 514 control and 4,623 treated.

Attrition was 15.4% treated (969/6,300) vs 18.9% control (132/700): −3.5 pp, 95% CI −6.5 to −0.4. That is a save rate of 0.18 (bootstrap CI 0.03 to 0.30), below the 0.25 assumed. Mean calibrated p on the pilot list was 0.21, inside the control interval (16.0–21.8%) but above the observed 18.9%, so dissatisfaction overstates 56-day attrition by about a tenth; on the model’s scale s = 0.035 / 0.21 ≈ 0.17. And 44% took the waiver, so an offer really cost $27.60. Cost per incremental save: $790 (CI roughly $420 to $3,800).

Complaints moved the wrong way: 1.13% vs 0.86%, +0.27 pp (CI −0.46 to +1.00), on 6 control events. The CLV split was worse news. Below $1,000 CLV, attrition was 24.5% treated (n = 2,710) vs 26.0% control (n = 300), −1.5 pp (CI −6.7 to +3.7). Above $1,000 it was 8.5% (n = 3,590) vs 13.5% (n = 400), −5.0 pp (CI −8.5 to −1.5). The interaction is not significant (p = 0.27), so it is a hypothesis, not a targeting rule.

Business impact

The instruction changes from “call the top 10%” to “call whoever clears the EV bar, priced with s and c from the latest holdout.” Re-priced at s = 0.17 and $27.60, the rule shrinks to 296 customers (1.9%) and nets +$11.3K on the holdout, about $74K of 3-year margin per 100K scored. Top-decile calls at the same inputs lose $14.8K, and use five times the agent hours.

The re-priced list breaks even at s = 0.071, inside the pilot’s interval: enough to adopt the rule, not to scale it. A deployment would keep a permanent 10% holdout and re-estimate s quarterly, and wave two would stratify by CLV band with about 1,500 control customers to decide whether the under-$1,000 segment stays on the list.

Technical deep dive

Data & pipeline. Same cleaning as the course project (constant and duplicate drops, sentinel flags, zero counts, var38/var15 features; 318 → 165 columns), with drop decisions made on the development split only.

Model and rules. Same XGBoost/LightGBM setup, no class weighting: scale_pos_weight lowered AUC in the course work, and leaving it at 1 keeps raw scores closer to probabilities. The team’s under-23 override (score = 0) became an explicit eligibility rule the retention team owns.

Metric definitions. Attrition: account closure, primary-product cancellation, or ≥80% balance outflow within 56 days of assignment. Save rate: (control − treated attrition) / control attrition, or divided by mean predicted p when it feeds the rule.

Evaluation. Stratified 5-fold OOF for the model and calibrator; one look at the locked holdout; 1,000 bootstrap resamples for policy intervals. Imbalance is handled by calibration, not reweighting. The Kaggle snapshot has no dates, so the split is random and drift had to be simulated. The pilot had one pre-registered readout; guardrails were checked weekly, with a stop rule at complaints above twice control.

Failure cases. - Score drift. In a simulated later snapshot (var15 shifted +2 years, var38 mode share raised from 19.6% to 25%), score PSI reached 0.18 and ECE 0.017. At the same threshold the rule offered 31% more customers, many below break-even. Monthly ECE/PSI checks trigger a calibrator refit on the latest labeled window. - Unreadable reasons. SHAP’s top features, such as saldo_var30, mean nothing on a call. I grouped SHAP values by column-name family (balances, counts, indicators, age-like var15) into two plain-language codes; agents would still find them thin. - Isotonic plateaus. Sparse positives in the extreme tail produce flat steps, and ties near the threshold made list size jump by dozens between refits. I pre-binned the score to at least 200 rows per step.

What I’d optimize next. A save rate per CLV band once wave two supports it; a day-180 readout, because valuing a day-56 save at 3-year CLV assumes the save lasts; and an uplift model after two or three quarters of holdout data.

If I made this real

  • Get labeled data with real margin and timestamps, so CLV is observed and the split can be time-based.
  • Replace the assumed offer cost and acceptance rate with actual campaign costs.
  • Run the 10% holdout for at least two cycles, with a pre-registered analysis, before trusting s.
  • Test with agents whether the reason codes change what they say on calls.
  • Re-check calibration on an out-of-time sample before any threshold goes live.
