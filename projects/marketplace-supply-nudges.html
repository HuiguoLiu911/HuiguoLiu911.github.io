Concept extension of Demand Sense · Zone forecasts, supply-gap nudges, switchback test

Real

In Spring 2026 a five-person Big Data Analytics team (Team 7) built an 11-stage Databricks pipeline (PySpark, Delta Lake) over 9.4M NYC yellow-taxi trips from January to March 2023 that ranked zones per time window with a heuristic demand score, turned them into GPT-4o-mini driver nudges with automated grounding checks, and reported a temporal holdout hit rate; I owned the LLM nudge phase.

Business context

A ride-hail marketplace pays twice for one mismatch: drivers idle where demand has already peaked while riders three zones away wait or give up. The operator pays in online-hour incentives, abandoned requests and drivers who quit when hourly earnings slip.

This extends a course project in which our team ranked NYC taxi zones by historical demand and had an LLM explain the ranking; I wrote the nudge layer and co-owned the evaluation. I kept one premise: drivers decide, and are told why. The pilot below is simulated.

The problem

“Midtown is busy” is useless if forty idle drivers are already there. The question is where demand will exceed nearby free supply in 30–45 minutes, and whether nudging drivers there changes outcomes.

Primary metric: driver utilization, paid minutes (pickup to dropoff) ÷ online minutes per 2-hour block. With demand fixed, an extra paid minute is a request that would otherwise have gone unserved, so this is the supply side of the north-star, completed trips.

Guardrails, limits fixed in advance:

  • Earnings per online hour (gross fares ÷ online hours): may not fall more than 2%.
  • Driver cancellation rate (cancellations after acceptance ÷ accepted dispatches): may not rise more than 0.5 pp.
  • Nudge opt-out rate (drivers who turned nudges off by day 28 ÷ drivers nudged): must stay under 4%.

Secondary: median pickup ETA in weekday peaks.

The original pipeline ignored supply and was judged by a hit rate I helped design and have since retired: 8 of 8 time windows, a 95% interval of 63–100%, on zones so stable that persistence would likely score the same.

What I built

Forecast. LightGBM predicts pickups per zone per 15-minute slot, 30–45 minutes ahead, for the 180 zones averaging at least 20 trips a day. Features: calendar, hourly weather, same slot last week, the slot’s two-week mean, and pickups and dropoffs in the zone and its neighbors over the last 45 minutes. A Tweedie objective handles zero-heavy slots; a quantile model gives P80.

Supply-gap score. Gap = P80 forecast minus expected free vehicles (idle drivers plus dropoffs completing in the zone). P80, not the median, because an unfilled gap loses a trip while an overfilled one costs a few empty minutes. A nudge fires when the gap is at least 4, the zone is within 10 minutes, and the driver has had no nudge for 30 minutes. Each gap goes to at most gap ÷ expected follow rate drivers.

Explainable nudge. Before the LLM is called, a record holds the zone, deadhead minutes, forecast range, nearby supply versus typical, and one of six reason codes mapped from the largest SHAP contributions (“rain in the next hour”). gpt-4o-mini rewrites this into at most 40 words. Checks require every number to appear in the record and the zone name to match, and reject banned terms (surge, guaranteed, dollar amounts). A failure or a 3-second timeout ships a fixed template. With six codes a template covers most cases, so the LLM stays only if it beats the template on follow rate in a rollout arm.

I rejected a graph or ConvLSTM model, which three months over 180 zones cannot support, and automated repositioning, which removes driver choice and needs dispatch integration.

Generation runs in the 15-minute batch (p95 1.9 s per call). At about 410 input and 55 output tokens a call costs roughly $0.0001; caching on zone, code and rounded numbers cut 220,000 nudges to about 30,000 calls, under $3. TLC data holds no driver identities. A 2% daily sample, plus every fallback, goes to human review.

Results

The pilot is an agent-based replay of March 4–31, 2023 demand (2.9M trips as requests, 16 paid minutes on average) with 8,000 synthetic drivers and about 1.5M online hours. Follow probability, its decay and opt-out behavior are inputs I set, so the pilot tests whether the design can detect and price an effect this size, not how real drivers respond.

Design. A city-level switchback: 336 two-hour blocks over 28 days, balanced so each week is 42/42 and every slot of the day is treated on 14 days. Control blocks show the existing static heatmap. Driver randomization was out (nudged drivers take trips controls would have had) and zone clusters leak across boundaries; the switchback’s cost is carryover, so each block’s first 30 minutes are dropped. An A/A run on the February replay gave a block residual SD of 2.5 pp and a standard error of 0.30 pp (0.27 under independence, plus 10% for autocorrelation): MDE ≈ 0.85 pp at α = 0.05, power 0.8. A day-10 look covered guardrails only; the primary was read once at day 28 (block-level OLS, week and slot fixed effects, Newey–West errors, permutation check).

Primary. Utilization rose from 51.8% to 52.9%: +1.1 pp (95% CI +0.5 to +1.7), +2.1% relative, permutation p < 0.001. Control blocks right after a treatment block were not measurably higher (+0.2 pp, CI −0.6 to +1.0), so the washout looks long enough.

Decay. Week 1 showed +1.9 pp; weeks 3 and 4, +0.6 and +0.7, as the share of nudged drivers who moved within 15 minutes fell from 31% to 19%. The trend alone is not significant (p ≈ 0.11) and I built it into the simulator, but four weeks cannot tell a steady +0.65 pp from zero.

ETA. In the 80 weekday peak blocks (06–10, 16–20), median pickup ETA fell 0.4 min from 6.4 (CI −0.7 to −0.1); across all blocks, −0.2 min (CI −0.4 to 0.0).

Late night. In the 56 blocks from 00:00 to 04:00 the effect was +0.1 pp (CI −1.4 to +1.6). WAPE there is 0.33, and a 10-minute deadhead for one uncertain trip rarely pays.

Guardrails. Earnings per online hour rose 1.6% (CI −0.2% to +3.4%), though empty driving rose 4%, a fuel cost the gross figure hides. Cancellations moved the wrong way: 3.1% to 3.4% (+0.3 pp, block-level CI +0.1 to +0.5, about 1.0M accepted dispatches per arm), so the upper bound touches the limit. Drivers heading to a nudged zone were declining pickups that pointed the other way. Opt-out reached 2.6% of the 7,400 drivers who got the pilot’s 220,000 nudges, mostly those receiving more than six a day.

Business impact

I would ship a scoped rollout with a holdout, not a full launch: every hour except 00:00–04:00, five nudges per driver per day, and text telling drivers to keep accepting trips on the way. I plan around the weeks 3–4 level, +0.65 pp (CI −0.2 to +1.5). Rounded down to +0.6 pp on the roughly 1.4M online hours per four weeks outside late night, that is about 8,400 paid hours, or 31,000 completed trips that would otherwise have gone unserved.

Because that interval includes zero, 25% of blocks stay held out for twelve weeks, enough for 80% power at +0.6 pp. A cancellation rise above +0.5 pp at any weekly read stops the rollout; at week 12, nudges stay only if utilization is up at least +0.3 pp.

Technical deep dive

Data and pipeline

9.4M trips, cleaned and aggregated in PySpark into a Delta table of 180 zones × 96 slots × 90 days (1.56M rows), joined to hourly NOAA weather. Deadhead times are median zone-to-zone durations by hour.

Model and rules

LightGBM with Tweedie variance power 1.3 and early stopping on validation. Gap thresholds and caps were tuned on a replay of the validation window, never on March 4–31.

Metric definitions

WAPE = Σ|actual − forecast| ÷ Σ actual over zone-slots, so busy zones dominate, as they do for nudges. Dispatch-level rates are aggregated to blocks, the randomized unit, before inference.

Evaluation

Time split: train January 15–February 19 (622K rows), validate February 20–March 3, test March 4–31 (484K rows); early January is lag history only, and every feature is available at issue time. Test WAPE: same-slot-last-week 0.28, two-week mean 0.25, LightGBM 0.21; the 60 quietest zones sit at 0.39. P80 covered 77.6% of slots, slightly overconfident. Gaps are about 6% of test zone-slots, so I report precision: in the simulator, 64% of flagged gaps saw requests exceed free supply by at least 2.

For the text, I labeled 400 structured reasons, stratified by code and zone tier, on a four-point rubric (grounded, right zone, readable, no implied certainty); a second reviewer labeled 100 (κ = 0.78). Checks passed 385 (96.3%); the 15 failures (rounding 9, second zone 4, banned word 2) fell back to the template. The 385 contained 2 unsupported causes (0.5%).

Failure cases

  1. Herding. Before the cap, a LaGuardia gap drew three times the drivers it needed and flipped to oversupply within 20 minutes. The follow rate behind the cap decays, so it is re-estimated weekly.
  2. Invented causes. Both hallucinations named an event (“a game tonight”) when the code said only weekend evening. Number checks miss this; a per-code whitelist of cause phrases would catch it.
  3. Weather leakage. The backtest uses observed weather; forecast-sized noise on precipitation raised rainy-slot WAPE by about 0.02.

What I’d optimize next

Match drivers to gaps jointly as a small assignment problem, and recalibrate P80 before the gap rule leans on it harder.

If I made this real

  • Get request logs and minute-level idle positions from an operator; TLC pickups are served demand, censored wherever supply ran short.
  • Replace the simulator’s follow, decay and opt-out assumptions with a small field test.
  • Run an A/A switchback on real traffic to measure block variance and carryover before trusting the MDE.
  • Test the nudge text in 10–15 driver interviews and re-label the eval set.
